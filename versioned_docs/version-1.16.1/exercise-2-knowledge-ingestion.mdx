## 🧠 Embeddings

When a document is ingested, it's transformed into a <b>vector representation</b> using an <i>embedding model</i>. These vectors help the AI <b>understand meaning and similarity</b> between pieces of text.

<div style={{ background: '#e0f2fe', border: '2px solid #38bdf8', borderRadius: '8px', padding: '0.8em 1.2em', margin: '1em 0' }}>
  Embeddings let the model know that <b>"bug report"</b> and <b>"defect ticket"</b> might refer to the same concept.
</div>

In this tutorial, we use the model <code>text-embedding-3-large</code> from Azure OpenAI.

<Explore title="Walk around an embedding space" href="https://projector.tensorflow.org/" source="Embedding Projector">
  <p><b>What you'll find:</b> 10,000 English words from a Word2Vec model, each one a point in a 200-dimensional space, squeezed into 3D so you can rotate it. Use the <b>Search</b> box on the right, click a word, and the panel lists its nearest neighbours.</p>
  <p><b>What to look for:</b></p>
  <ul>
    <li>Search <code>bug</code>. Its neighbours are <i>bugs, fix, patch, compiler, tracking</i>. Nobody told the model these words are related: it learned it from which words appear near each other in text.</li>
    <li>Search <code>test</code>. Its neighbours mix <i>testing</i> with <i>match, cricket</i> and <i>nuclear</i>. A word model has <b>one point per word</b>, so every meaning of "test" gets blended together.</li>
    <li>Switch between <b>UMAP</b>, <b>T-SNE</b> and <b>PCA</b> at the bottom left. The picture changes, but the neighbour list doesn't: the 3D view is only an approximation of the real 200 dimensions.</li>
  </ul>
  <p style={{ margin: 0 }}><b>How this connects to your knowledge base:</b> <code>text-embedding-3-large</code> works on the same idea, but it embeds whole chunks of text into 3,072 dimensions. Because it reads the surrounding words, "test" in a Jira issue lands near software testing, not cricket. When Dify retrieves chunks, it is finding the nearest points to your question in this kind of space.</p>
</Explore>

<Explore title="Why directions in the space carry meaning" href="https://www.3blue1brown.com/lessons/gpt#embedding" source="3Blue1Brown: Transformers, the tech behind LLMs">
  <p><b>What you'll find:</b> an animated lesson (video and text) on how LLMs work. The <b>Embedding</b> section is the clearest visual explanation of embeddings around. Best saved for after the workshop.</p>
  <p style={{ margin: 0 }}><b>What to look for:</b> the <b>Direction</b> part. The difference between <i>woman</i> and <i>man</i> is roughly the same arrow as the difference between <i>queen</i> and <i>king</i>. Meaning isn't just "which words are close": consistent directions encode concepts like gender or nationality. This is why similarity search works on meaning and not just on shared words.</p>
</Explore>

### 🏷️ Which Embedding Model?

The embedding model is chosen separately from the chat model you set up in Exercise 1. Two rules matter in practice:

<ul>
  <li><b>Choose for retrieval, on your own data.</b> A model that ranks well overall may not be the best at finding the right Jira issue. Exercise 2.1's benchmark is exactly this kind of check.</li>
  <li><b>Changing the embedding model means re-indexing everything.</b> Vectors from different models live in different spaces and can't be compared, so you can't mix them in one knowledge base.</li>
</ul>

<Explore title="Compare embedding models" href="https://huggingface.co/spaces/mteb/leaderboard" source="MTEB Leaderboard">
  <p><b>What you'll find:</b> the Massive Text Embedding Benchmark, the standard public ranking of embedding models, with separate leaderboards per task, language, and model size.</p>
  <p style={{ margin: 0 }}><b>What to look for:</b> open a <b>Retrieval</b> benchmark rather than the overall score, since retrieval is what RAG does. Compare the <b>size group</b> column: smaller models are cheaper and faster, and some are close to the big ones. Keep in mind that scores are self-reported and measured on public datasets, so treat the leaderboard as a shortlist, then test the candidates on your own documents.</p>
</Explore>

<Explore title="Further reading: embeddings in practice" href="https://simonwillison.net/2023/Oct/23/embeddings/" source="Simon Willison: Embeddings, what they are and why they matter">
  <p><b>What you'll find:</b> a readable article (and talk) that starts from "an embedding is a list of numbers" and ends with working semantic search and RAG.</p>
  <p style={{ margin: 0 }}><b>What to look for:</b> the <b>related content</b> example, where embeddings find similar articles with no keywords in common, and the section on <b>answering questions with RAG</b>. It's the same pipeline you're building in Dify: embed the documents, embed the question, retrieve the nearest chunks, and hand them to the LLM.</p>
</Explore>

<Explore title="Further reading: how embeddings are learned" href="https://jalammar.github.io/illustrated-word2vec/" source="Jay Alammar: The Illustrated Word2vec">
  <p><b>What you'll find:</b> an illustrated, step-by-step explanation of how Word2Vec, the model behind the Embedding Projector demo, learns its vectors.</p>
  <p style={{ margin: 0 }}><b>What to look for:</b> the opening <b>personality test</b> analogy, which shows how a person can be described as a list of scores and compared with <b>cosine similarity</b>. That's the same measure Dify uses to rank your chunks. Then skim the training section: the model learns by predicting which words appear next to each other, which is why <i>bug</i> ended up next to <i>fix</i>.</p>
</Explore>

## 🍰 Chunking: Breaking Text into Pieces

### 📏 Chunk Size

Your documents are too long to fit directly into the model's input window, so they are divided into <b>chunks</b>.

<table style={{ width: '100%', borderCollapse: 'collapse', marginBottom: '2em' }}>
  <thead>
    <tr style={{ background: '#f3f0ff' }}>
      <th style={{ padding: '0.5em', border: '1px solid #e5e7eb' }}>Chunk Size</th>
      <th style={{ padding: '0.5em', border: '1px solid #e5e7eb' }}>Pros</th>
      <th style={{ padding: '0.5em', border: '1px solid #e5e7eb' }}>Cons</th>
    </tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td style={{ border: '1px solid #e5e7eb' }}><b>Small Chunks</b> (200–300 tokens)</td><td style={{ border: '1px solid #e5e7eb' }}>🎯 High precision<br/>🔍 Matches narrow queries well</td><td style={{ border: '1px solid #e5e7eb' }}>🧩 May lose broader context</td></tr>
    <tr><td style={{ border: '1px solid #e5e7eb' }}><b>Large Chunks</b> (800–1000 tokens)</td><td style={{ border: '1px solid #e5e7eb' }}>📚 Preserve more context<br/>🔁 Fewer retrievals needed</td><td style={{ border: '1px solid #e5e7eb' }}>🧊 More noise<br/>🎯 Lower match precision</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>

<img src={require('../static/img/ex2/chunk-sizing.png').default} alt="Chunk Size Tradeoff" style={{ maxWidth: '100%', borderRadius: '8px', margin: '1em 0' }} />

<div style={{ background: '#fffbe8', border: '2px solid #facc15', borderRadius: '8px', padding: '0.8em 1.2em', margin: '1em 0' }}>
  Think of it like reading pages of a book — too many pages at once and the meaning blurs. Too few and you lose the plot.
</div>

### 🔄 Chunk Overlap

Overlap ensures that context near the boundaries of chunks isn't lost.

<img src={require('../static/img/ex2/chunk-overlap.png').default} alt="Chunk Overlap Visual" style={{ maxWidth: '100%', borderRadius: '8px', margin: '1em 0' }} />

For example, if two chunks overlap by 100 tokens, the second chunk repeats the last 100 tokens of the first. This helps the model "remember" what came before — improving answers that need continuity.

<div style={{ background: '#e0f2fe', border: '2px solid #38bdf8', borderRadius: '8px', padding: '0.8em 1.2em', margin: '1em 0' }}>
  <b>Tokens or characters?</b> The table above talks in tokens, but Dify's chunk settings are in characters. In English, one token is about 4 characters, so the 2,000-character chunks you'll use below are roughly 500 tokens.
</div>

<Explore title="See chunking happen" href="https://chunkviz.up.railway.app/" source="ChunkViz">
  <p><b>What you'll find:</b> paste any text, set <b>Chunk Size</b> and <b>Chunk Overlap</b> (in characters), pick a splitter, and every chunk is shown in its own colour.</p>
  <p><b>What to look for:</b> unzip the Jira dataset below and paste the first 40 lines or so of <code>REST_JiraEcosystem_issues.txt</code>.</p>
  <ul style={{ margin: 0 }}>
    <li>With the <b>Character Splitter</b> at a small chunk size, an issue's <code>key</code>, <code>summary</code> and <code>description</code> fall into different colours. A search that finds the summary won't bring back the key.</li>
    <li>Raise Chunk Size to <code>2000</code> and Overlap to <code>200</code>: whole issues now fit in one chunk, and the overlap repeats the edge of each chunk at the start of the next.</li>
    <li>Switch to the <b>Recursive Character Text Splitter</b>: it prefers to cut at blank lines and line breaks instead of mid-word. That's why you'll set a blank-line delimiter (<code>\n\n</code>) in Dify below.</li>
  </ul>
</Explore>

## 🎯 Ranking and top-k Retrieval

Once you have all your documents chunked and embedded, you'll want to find the most relevant ones to a query.

This is where <b>ranking</b> comes in:
<ul>
  <li>The <b>retriever</b> uses cosine similarity (or a similar metric) to score each chunk.</li>
  <li>Then it picks the <b>top K</b> chunks (usually 3–5) with the highest scores.</li>
  <li>These are sent to the LLM for final answer generation.</li>
</ul>

<div style={{ background: '#e0f2fe', border: '2px solid #38bdf8', borderRadius: '8px', padding: '0.8em 1.2em', margin: '1em 0' }}>
  <b>Getting the <code>top K</code> right can dramatically improve performance!</b>
</div>

Top K alone is not a quality target. Also test the retrieval mode,
score threshold, returned source, and behavior for an unrelated query. Exercise 2.1
uses the same Jira fixture to turn these controls into a repeatable benchmark.
